Stages of Stock Market Cycles

“The (Not So) Secret” that the “Big Players”
don’t want “Retail Investors” to know

Technical charts are often seen as tools used to make quick profits from stocks — for example, if the chart breaks out, buy and then wait to sell. But when people actually do it, they often run into false signals.

And in the end, they have to accept a stop loss as usual.

What I want to say is that “charts” can actually be used for much more than that. And if we truly understand charts deeply, we can adapt technical analysis to fit our own style.

And that will make investing much more effective.

(Not getting tricked, not getting hit hard.)

Stock prices are driven by only 2 things:

Demand (Bid)
Supply (Offer)

And prices rise when Demand is greater than Supply.

From the image, if you look from left to right, you’ll notice that:

⭐️ For prices to rise, “most of the shares” must be in the hands of the “owners.” ⭐️

This creates the first stage: Accumulation

This is the stage where the “big players” accumulate shares and suppress the stock price because they want to collect cheap shares. (This stage lasts a very long time.)

✨ This stage is suitable for investors who like cheap stocks and prefer stability. But you must know how to wait. ✨

Next, once the big players have accumulated enough shares,

they will push the stock price up, creating the Markup stage.

This is the phase where stocks rise quickly and aggressively. News and various “stories” usually appear during this stage. (This is the phase breakout traders love.)

✨ This stage is suitable for investors who like speed, don’t want to wait long, and want immediate results — win or lose. But you must know how to manage risk. ✨

Finally, once the big players are satisfied, they will begin selling shares.

(But they won’t dump everything at once, of course. They gradually sell because they hold a large amount.) This creates the Distribution stage.

The stock price will “move sideways within an upper range.”

In this stage, the only workable strategy is buying at the bottom and selling at the top of the range. (Trading within the range.)

✨ This stage is suitable for traders who are good at swing trading because the range tends to be quite clear. Losses are usually small, and gains are moderate.

But it also requires strong discipline and consistent homework. ✨

The final stage is the one that “most investors love.”

But in reality, it’s the stage that should be avoided the most.

That is the Markdown stage, because the stock will continue falling.

There may be temporary rebounds, but the overall destination is still downward.

Investors will be deceived by news and narratives such as:
“The stock is already very cheap,”
“It will recover,”
“Next year’s profits will be great,” and so on.

✨ This stage is suitable for people who want to become bag holders, lose money, and donate the hard-earned money they worked so hard for back to the stock market. In this stage, you don’t need discipline or homework — just trade based on “what you hear.” ✨

The difficulty is not in “knowing.”

The difficulty is in “distinguishing.”

⭐️ “Stocks with growing profits will always have an uptrend chart.” ⭐️
And stocks with an uptrend chart will always generate profits when traded 💸

My technique is to first screen for profitable companies, then look at which ones have “profit growth.”

First, you need to understand the basic structure of profit.

1. Profit comes from: Revenue minus Cost

(Total Profit = Total Revenue - Total Cost)

According to the income statement 📈📉

The top line is Revenue.

In simple terms, total revenue is called the Top Line ⏫

The middle section is Cost, called the Middle Line ↕️

The bottom line is Total Profit, called the Bottom Line ⏬

The structure looks like this:

Top Line = Revenue (Total Revenue)
🔽
Middle Line = Total Cost
🔽
Bottom Line = Profit (Total Profit)

Now back to the key point:
“A good stock is a stock with growing profits.”

But you also need to understand:
What is driving that profit growth???

1. The Best ⭐️⭐️⭐️
1.1 Profit grows because the Top Line increases while the Middle Line decreases

Top Line increasing = Sales are growing
(And you can go deeper to analyze whether growth comes from P or Q — both are good, but differently.)

Middle Line decreasing = If costs decline while sales are growing, it means the business is benefiting from EoS (Economies of Scale).

2. Good, but Secondary ⭐️⭐️
2.1 Profit grows because the Top Line increases, but the Middle Line also increases

(However, Revenue increases more than Cost increases = profits still grow.)

In this case, it can still be positive because sales are growing faster than costs.
And the rising costs may come from “selling expenses” or “branch expansion costs.”

(Not too concerning, because once the business starts benefiting from EoS or stops expanding branches, those costs will naturally decrease.)

Or,

2.2 Profit grows because the Top Line increases while the Middle Line stays flat

This is a continuation of case 2.1 — costs are starting to stabilize while revenue is still growing.
(The business is beginning to benefit from EoS, although still in the early stages, and most fixed costs are already covered.)

3. Neutral ⭐️
3.1 Profit grows because the Top Line stays flat (or declines), but the Middle Line decreases even more

In this case, the company is doing Cost Cutting because revenue growth is becoming difficult, so management shifts focus to controlling costs instead.

This situation is not always bad because some businesses are seasonal. During low seasons, cost cutting may be necessary.

But be careful with companies that used to grow strongly and suddenly start aggressive cost cutting.
That may indicate the company expects future revenue growth to become difficult.
(In most cases, the chart will eventually turn into a downtrend.)

Charts and financial statements are connected.
But you must learn how to identify which stage the business is currently in.
